Most early-stage SaaS companies share one problem: a product that works, and almost nobody who knows it exists. Paid ads can fix that, but they cost money before you know whether your message converts. Cross-promotion is the other route. Instead of buying attention, you borrow it from a company whose visitors already look like your customers.

## What SaaS cross-promotion means

Cross-promotion is an arrangement where two or more non-competing products show each other to their own audiences. It can take several forms:

- **Partner pages and integrations:** each product lists the other as a recommended tool.
- **Newsletter or social swaps:** each company mentions the other to its subscribers or followers.
- **Co-marketing:** a joint webinar, report or case study that both audiences see.
- **Ad swaps:** each site displays the other's ad, so each product is shown to the other's existing visitors.

What these have in common is reciprocity. You give some of your own audience's attention, and you receive some of theirs.

## Why it can work for early-stage SaaS

- **Warm context.** A visitor who sees a recommendation on a site they already use is in a different frame of mind than someone who meets a cold ad.
- **Neighbouring problem, different product.** The best partners solve a related problem without overlapping with you. An invoicing tool and a payroll tool can share customers without competing.
- **No per-click bill.** You pay with ad space and effort instead of cash. That helps when your budget is close to zero, although it is not free: placement, partner time and tracking all take work.

## Why it often fails

- **Poor fit.** Two audiences that look nothing alike produce clicks without signups.
- **Direct competitors.** Showing a rival to your visitors helps the rival. Every swap needs a rule that keeps competitors out.
- **Low-traffic partners.** A partner with few visitors cannot give you much, however enthusiastic they are.
- **No measurement.** If you cannot tell which partner sent which signup, you cannot decide whom to keep.
- **Generic outreach.** "Let's cross-promote!" says nothing about why your audiences overlap. A specific proposal is much easier to say yes to.

## How to evaluate a partner

Before you agree to anything, check four things:

1. **Audience overlap.** Would a typical user of their product plausibly need yours?
2. **No competition.** Do you sell to the same buyers for the same job?
3. **Real traffic.** Look at how many people actually visit the page where your ad or mention would appear, not only the site's total traffic.
4. **Visibility.** Will your placement be seen, or buried in a footer?

## Doing it by hand versus through a network

By hand, you find partners, negotiate each one, place the ads and compare numbers in a spreadsheet. That works at small scale and gives you full control, but the time cost grows with every partner.

## How to start this week

1. Write down your ideal customer in one sentence.
2. List ten products that same person already uses and that do not compete with yours.
3. Pick the three where you can explain, in two sentences, why their users would want your product.
4. Propose something concrete: a specific placement, for a specific period, with numbers you will both track.
5. Give every link its own tracking parameter so each partner's results can be told apart.
6. Review after a month and keep only the partners that sent real signups.

## What to measure

Views tell you the placement was seen. Clicks tell you the message was interesting. Signups and paying customers tell you the audience fits. Track all three for each partner, and judge an arrangement on signups, not clicks.

Cross-promotion will not replace every other channel, and it will not rescue a poor fit. Done carefully, with the right partners and honest measurement, it is one of the few ways to reach relevant visitors without an advertising budget.
